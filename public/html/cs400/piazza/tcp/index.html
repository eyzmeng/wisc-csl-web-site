How Computers Talk

(Not an answer to the Piazza question that sparked this; I've written that already. It was boring. :.)

Once upon a time there were computers. There were also telephones, telegraphs, which people used to talk to other people. People wondered if computers could also talk like them, perhaps over a similar sort of telephone wire...

You find the number you want to dial from your phone book first by flipping to the right section using an index; inside the section may be finer divisions; and finally you reach the number, dial it up. The other person picks up the phone, and... you got two people talking.

Okay, maybe that wasn't entirely accurate, and that most likely wasn't the single reason that sparked TCP/IP. But the analogy is close as far as I am concerned. Namely:

  • The phone book is DNS (Domain Name System), and the index would be DNS Servers;
  • The section would be the TLD (Top-Level Domain, that includes generic TLDs like .com, country-code TLDs like .me, and more);
  • The finer divisions by the way are second-level domains, The most famous one is perhaps .ac.uk, which is like the .edu in UK. There, .ac is the second-level domain, a finer division within the .uk country-code TLD. (In China we are less creative :P we just follow the same name as US: .edu.cn)
  • The phone number is the Internet Protocol address (known better as IP address);
  • The telephone line is like the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP). And the picking-up-phone would be "accepting" the connection.

Very much a flawed analogy, but what does carry over is their importance. Telephones revolutionized the way people communicate, just like the way the Internet revolutionized... the way computers communicate. Yeah that sounded better in my head. Anyway.

Getting IP Address

I'll talk about the IPv4 family, since like the softwares, my brain has not adapted to IPv6 yet…

In IPv4, it's important to know the blocks in which the loopback, private, and public addresses reside. I'd like to cite RFC, but... take this as "common sense" for now. (I'm looking at RFC 1918 and 5735 though)

127.0.0.0/8          - loopback, typically
(So 127.0.0.0 up to 127.255.255.255)
(This is roughly 256 addrs)

10.0.0.0/8           - private (Class A)
(So 10.0.0.0 up to 10.255.255.255)
(This is roughly 256^3 addrs)

172.16.0.0/12        - private (Class B)
(172.16.0.0 up to 172.31.255.255)
(This is roughly 256^2 x 16 addrs)

192.168.0.0/16       - private (Class C)
(So 192.168.0.0 up to 192.168.255.255)
(This is roughly 256^2 addrs)

I am saying roughly because you can't really use all addresses from what I read? I don't know? But those are the general ranges. Technically the entirety of 127/8 is loopback, but in practice it's just 127/32. Often, 127.0.0.1 is *the* loopback addresses, although we also call it localhost.)

I learned the importance of knowing the block of private address, because one time I entered 193.168.x.x instead of 192.168.x.x while copying the IP address from another computer, and when I tried to SSH into it my connection actually went through several network hops and ended up connecting to a Russian server... so yeah. There are some digits you are allowed to mess up when copying an IP address and your connection will stay in your local network: 192.168 are not one of them.

Oh yeah, I did mention getting the IP address. How'd you really do that though? Here are a few ways I do (not comprehensive nor guaranteed to work by any means):

  • ipconfig getifaddr en0 (on Mac; en0 is the name of the Ethernet interface)
  • ifconfig -a, then look for something like wlan0 (on BSD and Linux)
  • ip a, then look for something like wlan0 (on Linux; the command is short for ip address)

If all else fails (for example iSH Alpine on my iPhone) I like to do this funny thing where I create an HTTP server that can be accessed publicly:

python3 -m http.server -d /var/empty -b 0.0.0.0 8000

Then I visit http://10.x.x.x:8000, whatever the address of this HTTP server may be, and read the IP address from the access log... it's nasty but works for me :)

P.S. You can get your public address by using something like ifconfig.me or mxtoolbox.com/whatismyip/:

$ curl https://ifconfig.me
72.33.2.155

But in my opinion that's the less interesting part, since people from outside the network can't really access your computer from that address. (But that's my way of saying, "I don't know what you can do with this address". It could be useful for what it's worth.)

Domain Name System: Phonebook of the Internet

But before I go, I'll leave you with this: the silly domain names you see all around? Those are really public IP addresses in disguise. And this is no secret. Whether you try to access a web page:

$ telnet git.doit.wisc.edu 22

You will see that there is this conspicuous message at the top showing you the actual thing it is connecting to: an IPv4 address. How does it know the IPv4 address? Well, if my knowledge of DNS serves me right (and I have no sources to back up this one), it goes roughly like this:

  • First off, your computer performs a domain name analysis. It's like among computers, there is an agreed-upon convention for what are TLDs and what are not. Here in each of the domain, .edu is the TLD, wisc.edu is the apex domain name, while git.doit and pages.cs are the subdomains respectively. (The thing that I recall the inventor of the Web lamenting is the way domains are written backward, from specific to general instead of the other way around. But it's kind of like US/UK date format versus the ISO date, we just get used to it... well at least some of us do I believe. :.)
  • Then it asks the name server of the .edu TLD: "where is wisc.edu?" and it points to the name server of wisc.edu. And then it asks that "where is your subdomain pages.cs/git.doit?" And then it finally returns an _A record_ (A stands for address). The first two parts might be grossly inaccurate considering how universities are kind of their own boss (in other words wisc.edu may be its own name server... whatever the heck that means.) But for those of us mere mortals who own domains with generic TLDs like .org and .net, that is what happens: the .org TLD goes to Public Interest Registry while .net goes to Verisign. Then it goes to the registrar's name server because I am not my own name server; that is provided by my registrar.

Now, there is also the AAAA address, but like I said, I don't want to be thinking about IPv6 today. So A records will be all that matters. And yes, as the owner (loaner?) of a domain name, I too can control the A record... to some extent. For example, I made the "cs400-vm" subdomain point to the Google Cloud Compute machine:
