Why Unix? An extended answer

(In response to the totally unrelated Piazza question, "Why Linux", Post @75. I have zero motivation in re-organizing this into logical sections. Please do not email me to do that.)

So first off, what is Unix? For one, it is the name of the operating system made by Ken Thompson, Dennis Ritchie, Doug McIlory, Lee McMahan, and many others at Bell Labs near the end of 1960s (which itself was a pun on its source of inspiration (frustration?) Multics, where "Multi" is replaced with "Uni". This is to say, from the very beginning, Unix sought to be a simplicistic, lightweight, and somewhat wryly speaking, a "worse" Multics. To get an idea of how old Unix is, the Unix time roughly counts the number of seconds since its birth, which you can see by typing date +%s into your terminal. As of now, it sits at approximately 1.74 billion seconds, or about 55 years if you divide it by the 31.6 million seconds per year.)

On the other hand, it is the name of a trademark spelled with all capital letters (i.e. UNIX) which, according to Wikipedia, AT&T sold to Novell Inc., who later sold to the now-holder of the trademark, The Open Group. In that respect, a UNIX operating system means any operating system that complies by the Single UNIX Specification as certified by The Open Group, such as IBM AIX and macOS, while demoting the BSD lineage and Linux-based operating systems to a meager "Unix-like" status (To be fair, even given a chance to be UNIX, GNU wouldn't try to due to AT&T's proprietary say over the ownership of original UNIX code.)

The Unix I wanted to talk about is somewhat more abstract than either of these: Unix as the lessons, principles, and philosophies, summarized by these smart people who made it. Modularity is one thing: Unix programs tend to do one job --- atomic, as you might call it. But for a Unix program and the one job it does, it does that job very well. And one measure of well-ness is interoperability, i.e. how well it works with other programs. (Now I just found out that Brian Kernighan (who also worked on Unix at Bell Labs and famously co-authored the C Programming Language book) has given an elaborate example in the AT&T archives of how the Unix pipeline works, so I'll let him demonstrate instead of me giving a lengthy example. ;)

Oh, and there is another point I feel the need to stress: everything in Unix is text. (Now, the idea of text and the fact that it is not a fundamental concept to computers and requires an encoding to be interpreted can be foreign to people who simply understand text as a Microsoft Word document --- at least in where I'm from. But since you've all written plenty of Java code which is 100% text, I think everyone should be comfortable with me using that as the analogy.) So, just to give you an idea of how much of Unix is text: the user database is text (try less /etc/passwd), configurations are text (if you've set up your Google Cloud machine, try less /etc/ssh/sshd_config as root), command output is text, command input is text... of course for some commands the input/output can be arbitrary data, but all Unix programs speak in text. (Of course you may have heard of everything in Unix is a file, from the console standard input/output/error to block devices of mass storage devices... but I'm really just emphasizing the text aspect/format of Unix's interface here.)

These are all in very sharp contrast with how things tend to be done in the Windows/Mac world: monoliths that do multiple things at once (not atomic/modular) and walled-gardens that do not collaborate well with other software (lack of interoperability, or worse, vendor lock-in). Of course, people normally don't pay much attention to how much RAM the constant system update checks Windows runs in the background costs (unless they happen use an old laptop that should be obsolete in the 2020s (definitely not talking about me lol)), or how Windows and Mac race to make the "intelligent" operating system at the expense of adding more overloaded functionalities and essentially nullifying user privacy and control over their own stuff. But these are the precise things that make the two tech oligarchs intolerable for people who care about softwares that can be plugged into other programs, who care about learning the skills to build our own environment from these tiny modular pieces to meet our needs; and the freedom to apply that skill to any tools that we might come across (or even create ourselves) without limiting us to a specific everything-app that fulfills every demand out of the box, yet we don't know how to live without because we learn nothing from those monolithic apps and how it fulfills those demands. While Windows and Mac may be driven by practical ambitions to create the most convenient out-of-box experience, and hide the nitty-gritty details from end user, the Linux/BSD community has more of a DIY mentality: a nerdy obsession to reduce things down to their very essence, and keep that and nothing else; and a stubborn spirit to do things not because everything is neatly arranged and we're told to do such and such, but because we want to understand how things work inside-out.... and we want to be in control over our tools, our data, our digital home.

Though admittedly, towards the end, I am showing a bit of bias towards BSD because even Linux is slowly losing its Unix heritage (for reasons I don't understand, yet somehow resonate deeply with, systemd/journald was deemed to be Linux's most egregious deviation from the Unix philosophy. Oh, and remember I said that all Unix programs speak in text? Well journald's journal format overtly breaks that principle (I watched a video reviewing OpenBSD the other day and this comment is exactly how I felt XD) For that I am considering trying out something like FreeBSD, since while Linux-based systems are a hotch-potch of things that feel like Unix though not quite so, the BSD family are basically a fork of Bell Labs' Unix back when Ken returned to UC Berkeley, his alma mater, where he helped install a version of Unix that would become known as the Berkeley Software Distribution, or BSD. The quality and brevity of the man pages of BSD too seem to inherit the more pure Unix genes than Linux does (remember Multics, the revolutionary yet sophisticated system that Unix was specifically invented to replace? Yep, that's what I think the most important part of Unix is. Small is beautiful, less is more... if your man page takes more than hundreds of lines to document a program (note: this isn't quite the same as writing a general information/tutorial manual. I think there are separate places for those, like section 7 or something...) then it probably does too many things at once and should be broken down into smaller pieces.) I also heard that they have a superb handbook (the astute reader who pay attention to the hyperlink I reference for man pages in my various answers here may have noticed my recent inclusion of FreeBSD man pages over those of Arch Linux. Well... I guess you now know that it was by no means an accident. ;)
